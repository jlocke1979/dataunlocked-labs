The questions

Who plays, when they play, and what it means for the business.

Player behavior

Where records support it, examine repeat play, seasonal patterns, course preferences and spending. Understand how occasional golfers and frequent players use your facilities.

Course utilization

Explore patterns across the season and playing times. Where capacity and attendance can be established, distinguish busy periods, unused availability and booked rounds that were not played.

Membership economics

Examine how members and pass holders play, the revenue associated with their participation, and the tradeoffs between access, frequency and peak-time availability. Assess contribution only where relevant costs are available.

A focused pilot

Start with the exports. End with a decision.

  1. Review and scope. Review available tee sheet, membership and POS exports with your team. Confirm coverage, definitions, quality and whether records can be connected. Agree on a priority business question and a feasible scope before analysis begins.
  2. Build a credible baseline. Reconcile the usable records and analyze the agreed questions. Show the patterns supported by the data and document the gaps that limit interpretation.
  3. Compare choices. Explore relevant pass, membership or tee-time pricing scenarios. Make assumptions visible, including possible changes in participation. Separate observed results from hypothetical outcomes.
  4. Recommend the next step. Review findings with your team and identify practical actions. Where price response is uncertain, propose a measurable test or better data collection before a broader change.

What you receive

A findings brief, supporting tables and visuals, a documented set of scenarios, and a discussion of recommendations and limitations. Scope, timing and fee are agreed after export review. The engagement is consulting and analysis; it does not require a new operating platform.

Pricing with evidence

A scenario is not a demand forecast.

A higher price changes revenue per sale. The harder question is how many golfers will still buy, when they will play, and what they may choose instead.

Historical records may help estimate price sensitivity, but a relationship between price and rounds does not, by itself, establish cause and effect. Seasonality, weather, availability, promotions and the mix of players can also influence demand.

We first assess whether the evidence supports an elasticity estimate. Where it does, estimates should include uncertainty and validation. Where it does not, we show a range of explicit assumptions and outline a test with agreed comparison conditions, success measures and stopping rules. We do not promise a single optimal price or guaranteed revenue lift.
